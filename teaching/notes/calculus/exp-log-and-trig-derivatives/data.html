Section 2.4: A Handful of Derivatives

So far, we’ve defined derivatives with a cumbersome limit definition, and then found a cleaner formula for polynomials. We also know how to take derivatives of functions made by adding, subtracting, multiplying, or dividing other functions. There’s two major things left for us to do before we have a solid bedrock of derivatives: we need to know how to take derivatives of other basic functions, like $\sin(x)$, $\cos(x)$, $\ln(x)$, and $e^x$, and we also need to know how to differentiate functions made by combining others via composition: for example, $\cos\left( x^5 \right)$ or $(\cos(x))^5$.

Let’s save compositions for the next section and focus on derivatives of common functions for now, staring with the trig functions. The derivative of $\sin(x)$ is a little bit tricky to evaluate with the limit definition, but we can certainly do it! We have

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \left[ \sin(x) \right] = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin(x + h) - \sin(x)}{h},\end{alignat*}$$

which looks intractable until we focus in on the $\sin(x + h)$ term. While it might have been a while since you last saw it, the addition formula for $\sin$ can tell us that $\sin(x + h) = \sin(x)\cos(h) + \cos(x)\sin(h)$, so

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \left[ \sin(x) \right] &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin(x)\cos(h) + \cos(x)\sin(h) - \sin(x)}{h}\\&= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin(x)\cos(h) - \sin(x)}{h} + \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\cos(x)\sin(h)}{h}\\&= \sin(x) \left( \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\cos(h) - 1}{h} \right) + \cos(x) \left( \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin(h)}{h} \right),\end{alignat*}$$

and now both those limits are derivatives at zero! The former is the slope of $\cos(x)$ at $x = 0$, and the latter is the slope of $\sin(x)$ at $x = 0$. To understand both, we can return to the unit circle: these are the rates of change of the $x$- and $y$-coordinates when increasing from an angle of $0$ radians. At that point, the tangent line to the circle is vertical, so the rate of change of the $x$-coordinate is zero. That means all change in the angle is affecting the $y$-coordinate, and since the radian measure of an angle is its arc length in the unit circle, the rate of change of the $y$-coordinate is equal to the change in angle — in other words, its derivative is $1$.

With just this one derivative, we can go on to find the derivatives of the other five trig functions! First, we can find the derivative of $\cos$ with a slightly subtle approach involving the addition formula for $\cos$: we can write $\cos(x) = \sin\left( x + \frac{\pi}{2} \right)$ to express it in terms of a derivative we already know. While we haven’t yet discussed how to differentiate a composition of functions like this, we can once again use the addition formula for $\sin$ to get around the issue:

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \left[ \cos(x) \right] &= \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \left[ \sin\left( x + \frac{\pi}{2} \right) \right]\\&= \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} \left[ \sin\left( x \right)\cos\left( \frac{\pi}{2} \right) + \cos\left( x \right)\sin\left( \frac{\pi}{2} \right) \right]\\&= \cos\left( x \right)\cos\left( \frac{\pi}{2} \right) - \sin\left( x \right)\sin\left( \frac{\pi}{2} \right)\\&= \cos\left( x \right)\cos\left( \frac{\pi}{2} \right) - \sin\left( x \right)\sin\left( \frac{\pi}{2} \right)\\&= \cos\left( x + \frac{\pi}{2} \right)\\&= -\sin(x).\end{alignat*}$$

We’ll develop a much better approach to composed functions in the next section! The farther we can get from the limit definition of the derivative and obscure trig identities, the better. Thankfully, now that we have derivatives of both $\sin$ and $\cos$, we can fill in the rest of the trig functions with only the quotient rule.

Theorem: derivatives of trig functions

1. $\displaystyle \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} [\sin(x)] = \cos(x)$.

2. $\displaystyle \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} [\cos(x)] = -\sin(x)$.

3. $\displaystyle \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} [\tan(x)] = \sec^2(x)$.

4. $\displaystyle \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} [\sec(x)] = \sec(x)\tan(x)$.

5. $\displaystyle \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} [\csc(x)] = -\csc(x)\cot(x)$.

6. $\displaystyle \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x} [\cot(x)] = -\csc^2(x)$.

The notation $\sec^2(x)$ just means $(\sec(x))^2$ — we use it to distinguish from $\sec(x^2)$.

Example: derivatives of trig functions

Find $\displaystyle \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}[x^2\cos(x)]$.

This is a product of two functions we know how to differentiate: $x^2$ and $\cos(x)$. In total, we get

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}[x^2\cos(x)] = 2x\cos(x) + x^2(-\sin(x)).\end{alignat*}$$

While we’ve seen it from the limit definition, I think there’s still room to believe the fact that $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\left[ \sin(x) \right] = \cos(x)$ intuitively. At any point on the two graphs, the height of $\cos(x)$ gives the slope of $\sin(x)$, and the height of $\cos(x)$ is the opposite of the slope of $\sin(x)$. It’s a fundamental fact of waves that their slopes are given by other wave functions.

Example: a higher-order trig derivative

Find $\displaystyle \frac{d^{74}}{dx^{74}}[\cos(x)]$.

This seems pretty tricky on first glance, but it’s actually not so bad. Let’s write out the first few derivatives and see if we can spot a pattern.

$\displaystyle \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}[\cos(x)] = -\sin(x)$.

$\displaystyle \frac{\mathrm{d} ^2}{\mathrm{d} x^2}[\cos(x)] = -\cos(x)$.

$\displaystyle \frac{d^3}{dx^3}[\cos(x)] = \sin(x)$.

$\displaystyle \frac{d^4}{dx^4}[\cos(x)] = \cos(x)$.

Since the fourth derivative is just $\cos(x)$ again, every four derivatives we take won’t matter. Therefore, instead of taking the 74th derivative, we can take the 70th, or 66th, or 62nd, and so on. The largest multiple of 4 less than 74 is $72 = 4 * 18$, so

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}\frac{d^{74}}{dx^{74}}[\cos(x)] &= \frac{\mathrm{d} ^2}{\mathrm{d} x^2}[\cos(x)]\\&= -\cos(x).\end{alignat*}$$

Exponential and Logarithmic Derivatives

The final functions to talk about are exponential functions ($y = b^x$) and logarithmic ones ($y = \log_b(x)$). In case logs aren’t particularly familiar, let’s review them briefly. The log base $b$ of $x$ is the number you need to raise $b$ to in order to get $x$. For example, $\log_2(8) = 3$, since $8 = 2^3$. Of special importantance is the natural log, which is $\ln(x) = \log_e(x)$. That number $e$ is Euler’s constant: an irrational number defined as $e \approx 2.71828...$.

Theorem: the derivatives of exponential and logarithmic functions

Let $b > 0$. Then

1. $\displaystyle \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}[b^x] = b^x \cdot \ln(b)$.

2. $\displaystyle \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}[\log_b(x)] = \frac{1}{x \cdot \ln(b)}$.

In particular, when $b = e$, since $\ln(e) = 1$, we get

1. $\displaystyle \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}[e^x] = e^x$.

2. $\displaystyle \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}[\ln(x)] = \frac{1}{x}$.

This is really important: $y = e^x$ is the unique function that is its own derivative. In other words, the slope of the graph of $y = e^x$ is exactly its height.

This is one reason why we care so much about the number $e$: derivatives involving it are incredibly simple.

Example: the derivatives of exponential and logarithmic functions

Find the equation of the tangent line to $f(x) = x^2e^x$ at $x = 2$.

First of all, we need to find $f'(x)$. This is a Product Rule computation: $f'(x) = 2xe^x + x^2e^x$. For $x = 2$, $f(2) = 4e^2$ and $f'(2) = 8e^2$. Therefore, the equation of the tangent line is

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}y = 4e^2 + 8e^2(x - 2).\end{alignat*}$$
