Let be a smooth, oriented surface in with positively-oriented boundary , and let be a vector field whose components have continuous partial derivatives on and around . Then

In other words, the circulation around the boundary of is equal to the integral of the curl across .

Stokes’ Theorem is intuitively true for the same reason Green’s Theorem is: dotting the curl vector with the surface’s normal vector gives the infinitesimal circulation of in the surface at each point, and when we integrate those infinitesimal circulations, interior ones cancel out, leaving only the circulation on the boundary. If there’s one new thing we can take from Stokes’ theorem, it’s that we made a good choice with the generalization of curl to ; it may be unusual that it became a vector while divergence remained a scalar, but this theorem provides strong evidence that this was correct.

Incompressible Fields and Surface-Independence

In many ways, Stokes’ Theorem is the most direct higher-dimensional analogue of the Fundamental Theorem of Curve Integrals. That let us express a circulation integral of a field in terms of a potential function on the boundary of the curve, but only if such a function with existed. That was the case if was conservative, meaning , and for such fields, the theorem showed that their circulation was path-independent, depending only on the endpoints of any path taken.

Now, Stokes’ Theorem lets us express the flux of a field through a surface in terms of the circulation of a different field on the boundary, but only if such a field with exists. To keep the analogy going, let’s define terms equivalent to potential function, conservativity, and path-independence for this new situation.

Theorem: divergence-zero fields have vector potentials

Let be a vector field defined on all of . If , then has a vector potential.

This property will serve as our equivalent to conservativity, so let’s give it a name. Conservative came from conservation of energy — the stereotypical conservative field is gravity — but the standard name for a divergence-zero field stems from interpreting it as a fluid. If the divergence is zero, then there’s just as much flowing into any small region per second as flowing out of it.

Definition: incompressible field

A vector field is incompressible if everywhere.

We’re using rather than since this definition works just as well for 2D fields, but we’ll only be using it for 3D ones from here on out. As an example, a fluid like water is incompressible because it has constant density, which guarantees the divergence is always zero (otherwise positive-divergence pockets would be decreasing in density and vice versa).
