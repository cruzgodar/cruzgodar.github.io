Section 3.1: Riemann Sums

The concepts of Calculus I that we just reviewed — limits and derivatives — are incredibly useful, with wide-ranging applications across all areas of science. The derivative in particular is a tool that lets us compute the rate of change of a function; for example, the velocity of an object given its position. Frequently, though, we want to go the other way. How could we compute position from velocity?

This example led us directly to the idea that net change in position is the area under the graph of velocity. In other words, just like the slope of the tangent line to a graph was its rate of change, the area under a graph of a derivative between and gives the net change . There’s a lot of work between us and a general method to find the area under complicated graphs, but we have all the tools we need to build it. For rectangles and triangles, finding the area under a graph wasn’t too difficult, but for a more general function, it’s not even clear where we’d start. Just like with derivatives, we’ll start by approximating the area, and then taking the limit of better and better approximations. With derivatives, approximations meant slopes of secant lines, but here, just writing down what the approximations are will take a bit more work.

Riemann Sums

Sums are useful in general, but in this class, we need them for one specific purpose: approximating the area under curves. We’ll do this one axis at a time, starting with what needs to happen on the -axis: regular partitions.

Definition: regular partition

A regular partition of the interval is a set of numbers such that , , , and all of the are an equal distance from each other.

This is a really dense definition, but it’s not so bad in practice. We’ll think of regular partitions as living on the -axis of a graph, so a regular partition just chops up the interval into equal-sized pieces.

Okay, so what are we going to do with these? Let’s return to our original goal of approximating the area under a curve — we’re going to do that with a lot of rectangles.

So what? Well, just like we defined the derivative to be the slope of a tangent line, but we actually computed it by taking the limit of slopes of secant lines, we’re going to define another object that gives the area under a curve, and then we’ll compute it by taking the limit of Riemann sums as the number of subintervals approaches infinity. Also like derivatives, the limit definition is going to be a major pain to actually use, so we’ll spend much of the course building formulas and rules that let us compute that area without going to so much trouble.
