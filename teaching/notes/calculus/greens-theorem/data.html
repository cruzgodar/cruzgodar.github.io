Section 6.4: Green's Theorem

This section is an interesting one — in many multivariable calculus courses, it becomes something of a transitional fossil, since results we develop in later sections will supersede this one. However, I’d like to make the case that Green’s Theorem not only stands on its own as a useful theorem, but is also one of the most conceptually valuable results we’ll see in calculus. It’s the simplest version of a series of three results we’ll develop over the remaining sections, and that gives it the distinction of being as straightforward as possible to learn and remember while still containing nearly all of the conceptual nuance we’ll see in the future sections’ theorems.

While we certainly didn’t introduce them in such a manner, the Fundamental Theorems of both Calculus and Curve Integrals were closer to Green’s Theorem than they might seem at first: both equated a function evaluated on the boundary of a set (either an interval or a path in space) to the integral of its derivative over the set. In the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, the relationship between the function and its derivative was straightforward enough: even if the theory of differentiation took a long time to develop, a function and its derivative were both functions of one variable, and every continuous function had an antiderivative. In the Fundamental Theorem of Curve Integrals, that relationship became more distant: the derivative of a function of multiple variables is its gradient, which is a vector field, and not every function of multiple variables even has an antiderivative.

Green’s Theorem abstracts the situation further. There’s still a relationship between a function on the boundary of a set and the integral of its derivative over the set, but since the boundary is an entire set itself, “evaluating the function on the boundary” means taking an integral. In that respect, we can think of Green’s Theorem as being the third member of a family of theorems that increase the number of integrals present by one by taking a derivative; that is, treating the expression in the formula

as evaluating on the set , where the number of integrals has been increased from zero on the left to one on the right.

Flux

Before we can dig into the integrand in Green’s Theorem, we need to explore the theorem itself more: our story won’t be complete without an alternate perspective on it.

When we introduced circulation integrals in the previous section, we chose to express them in the language of curve integrals of functions of multiple variables (by taking a dot product) rather than developing something entirely new for vector fields. The Fundamental Theorem of Curve Integrals and Green’s Theorem proved that approach to be a good one: the two types of curve integrals are intrinsically linked. Those results also showed that the unit tangent vector in particular was a good choice of vector to dot with in order to make the translation back to a function of multiple variables.

It’s not the only choice we could make, though. Our motivating question in the previous section was asking how much a river current helped or hindered a swim, which led us to the notion of circulation, but let’s ask a different question: how much water flows through a net suspended in the river per second?

To head some concerns off at the pass, this is a fully 2D picture and situation; our goal is just to measure the amount of field flowing across the curve per unit time. This quantity is still computable with a curve integral, but rather than take a dot product of the field with the unit tangent vector at each point on the curve, we want a unit vector that’s orthogonal to the curve.

An appropriate name would be a unit normal vector, and we’ll make a definition with that name in just a moment, but there’s another subtlety to think through. For a circulation integral to work properly, the path had to be oriented. Reversing the orientation of reversed its unit tangent vector at each point, negating the entire integral; intuitively, swimming backward along a path means the amount the current helps or hinders your swim is exactly reversed.

In the same sense, any unit vector we choose that’s orthogonal to the curve will only be one of two possible vectors, and unlike the unit tangent vector, which always points in the direction the curve is oriented, there isn’t so natural a choice for whether a straight vertical line segment parameterized from bottom to top should have unit normal vectors pointing to the left or the right. Regardless of which we pick, one direction of field flow across the curve will be counted as positive and the other negative (since the dot product with the field will be negative).

We can make a more informed choice for the direction of a unit normal vector by talking about oriented boundaries. For a closed loop , we’ll define positive field flow to be crossing from inside to outside, and negative flow to be from outside to inside. Since an oriented boundary always keeps the region on the left, our unit normal vector will always point to the right, away from the region. This choice is consistent for any oriented path, not just boundaries of regions, which is a very nice side effect. With the goal in mind, let’s make some definitions!

It can be a little overwhelming to keep the notions of circulation, flux, curl, and divergence distinct; to disambiguate them, circulation and curl measure rotational motion, while flux and divergence measure expanding motion. Most importantly, though, circulation and flux are only defined relative to paths (or surfaces, as we’ll see later) through a vector field, whereas curl and divergence are properties of the field itself. These statements are a perfect capstone to this section — they set the stage for the final four that follow it. Our goal for the remainder of multivariable calculus is to bring Green’s Theorem to , and now that we’ve defined curl and divergence, we can see the outline of what that will look like. The curve integral part of the circulation form of Green’s Theorem will move to just fine — we’ve already been taking integrals along curves in with no issue. However, those curves are supposed to be the boundary of something in 3D space, and that no longer needs to be a flat region like it did in . Instead, it could be an arbitrary surface, and so we’ll need to develop a theory of integrating over surfaces. That will come in the next section, and the Green’s Theorem analogue in the one after that.

The flux form of Green’s Theorem is more complicated. As we alluded to earlier, there is no such thing as a single normal vector to a curve in ; that property is held instead by surfaces (for example, planes have normal vectors in , but lines do not). The flux integral in Green’s Theorem will therefore need to be replaced by a surface integral. Moreover, that surface is supposed to be the boundary of something, which will have to be an entire solid region of . Since there’s no extra dimension for it to curve into like with surface integrals, objects like these will be flat in a sense, and we’ll be able to handle them with a direct analogue of double integrals by just integrating one additional time.
