Surface Integrals of Vector Fields

Just like with curve integrals, we can slightly adapt surface integrals to be of vector fields instead of functions: we just need to take a dot product of the field with a vector on the surface. In , we could choose either the tangent vector or the normal vector to a curve to compute either the circulation along it or the flux through it, but a point on a surface in doesn’t have a single unit tangent vector, since there are infinitely many directions tangent to any given point on a smooth surface. However, there are still well-defined and (nearly) unique normal vectors! We’ve already computed them, too: for a surface parameterized by , the normal vector is .

There’s one new subtlety that appears with surfaces when we try to assign a normal vector to each point. With curves, knowing a parameterization provided an orientation to the curve, so long as was defined and nonzero: the positive direction was given by at each point. Since we effectively defined the normal vector to a curve in terms of the tangent vector, there were no problems there, either.

Have a look at this surface, though — it’s the famous Möbius strip, constructed by gluing a strip of paper into a loop with a twist. The red vector is normal to the surface; change the value of to slide it along until it’s eventually at the same point where it started.

It’s upside-down! This surface has the remarkable property that it’s impossible to choose a unit normal vector at every point so that they change continuously. That would foil our attempts to integrate using those normal vectors, so let’s give this notion a name so that we can focus only on the surfaces that don’t misbehave like this.

Definition: orientable surface

A surface in is orientable if there is a choice of unit normal vector for each point on , so that is a continuous function on .

Aside: orientability and curves

Informally, the Möbius strip only has one side instead of two, despite being fashioned from a two-sided strip. However, I think leaving the discussion there and just ignoring nonorientable surfaces for the remainder of the course leaves too large a loose end to be acceptable: why didn’t we need to work through all of this for curves?

The answer is somewhat subtle. If we’re given the parameterization of a curve, then there is no ambiguity over the unit tangent vector at each point, so long as the derivative exists and is nonzero. That rules out things like cusps and stalling at a point for some time, but not self-intersections. And indeed, a parameterized curve can intersect itself without causing an orientation issue: for example, the following curve in always has a well-defined unit tangent vector, even at the origin, since it just crosses through there at two different points in time.

Since the definition of orientability wants direction as a function of coordinates on the curve or surface, not parameters, the purple curve itself isn’t orientable, strictly speaking. If we forget the parameterization, we have no idea what direction to assign the origin, even with notions like positive orientation. That’s due in part to the self-intersection, but if we remove it by only defining the purple curve for (try adjusting and ), then we can still see the issue: there’s a cusp at the origin, so we’re not going to be able to vary the unit tangent vector smoothly. In effect, there is no way to join up one end of a smooth curve to another in a way that makes the unit tangent vectors (and therefore the unit normal vectors) discontinuous without that discontinuity showing up in the curve itself.

The Möbius strip is a lot like this curve. It’s effectively intersecting itself at the point where the red vector flips, and there’s a sense in which it should be drawn an entire second time to complete the parameterization and return to its original orientation. In that sense, the parameterization of the Möbius strip has a well-defined normal vector at pair of parameters , but not the surface itself. And the difference between surfaces and curves is that the extra parameter in surfaces affords them room to rotate and do what curves cannot: reconnect with themselves in a way that breaks the continuity of the normal vectors, but not the surface itself.

When we considered closed curves in in the previous section, a positive orientation on the curve induced normal vectors that pointed outward. For surfaces, we start with normal vectors themselves rather than tangent vectors, but we can do exactly the same thing: the positive orientation of a closed orientable surface is the choice of unit normal vectors that is outward at every point, like a porcupine.

We’ll primarily find the positive orientation of surfaces useful in the next section. For now, let’s get to the definition of a surface integral through a vector field; it’s a direct blending of a typical surface integral with a curve integral measuring flux.
