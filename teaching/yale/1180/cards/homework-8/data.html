12. Let $R : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ be a function (not necessarily a linear transformation) defined by rotating its inputs $90^\circ$ counterclockwise. For example, $R\left( \left[\begin{array}{c} 1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right] \right) = \left[\begin{array}{c} 0 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]$ and $R\left( \left[\begin{array}{c} 0 \\ -2 \end{array}\right] \right) = \left[\begin{array}{c} 2 \\ 0 \end{array}\right]$.

Problems 13–16 concern the following situation. A beekeeper keeps $h$ beehives next to an orchard with $a$ acres of apple trees, and the two businesses help each other synergistically. For the amount of production these businesses are considering, the function $T$ sending $\left[\begin{array}{c} h \\ a \end{array}\right]$ to $\left[\begin{array}{c} \text{pounds of honey} \\ \text{bushels of apples} \end{array}\right]$ is a linear transformation. Two years ago, $10$ hives and $20$ acres produced $500$ pounds of honey and $1040$ bushels of apples; last year, $20$ hives and $10$ acres produced $700$ pounds of honey and $580$ bushels of apples.

13. Find the matrix for $T$. Interpret each of its four entries in context, and identify which two entries represent one business benefiting from the other.

14. Show that the columns of the matrix for $T$ form a basis for $\mathbb{R}^2$. The two businesses would like to produce exactly $1000$ pounds of honey and $2080$ bushels of apples next year. Express that vector in this basis, and interpret the coefficients.

15. Honey sells for $\$5$ per pound and apples for $\$20$ per bushel. Maintaining $h$ hives costs the beekeeper $5h^2$ dollars per year, and maintaining $a$ acres costs the orchard $5a^2$ dollars per year (both grow faster than linearly, since expanding means using worse locations). Find the beekeeper’s profit $\pi_B(h, a)$ and the orchard’s profit $\pi_O(h, a)$. If each business chooses only its own variable to maximize its own profit, how many hives and acres will there be? Then find the values of $h$ and $a$ that maximize the combined profit $\pi_B + \pi_O$, using the second derivative test to verify it’s a maximum. Which business ends up too small when they act separately, and why?

16. When one business’s decisions affect another business’s profits and it doesn’t take that into account, the effect is called an externality (here, a positive externality). One fix is for the orchard to pay the beekeeper a fee of $s$ dollars per hive per year. Find the fee $s$ that makes the beekeeper’s most profitable number of hives equal the one from question 15, and show that it’s equal to the partial derivative of $\pi_O$ with respect to $h$. Explain in a sentence why that makes sense. (Pollination fees like this are a real and large industry — almond growers in California rent well over a million hives every spring.)
