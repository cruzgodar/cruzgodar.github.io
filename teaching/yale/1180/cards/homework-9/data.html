In problems 1–4, do the following:

a) Find the matrix for the given transformation $T$.

b) Determine if $T$ is one-to-one. If it is not, find a basis for $\ker T$.

c) Determine if $T$ is onto and find a basis for $\operatorname{image} T$.

d) If $T$ is invertible, find a formula for $T^{-1}$.

1.

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}T\left( \left[\begin{array}{c} x \\ y \end{array}\right] \right) = \left[\begin{array}{c} -2x + y \\ -5x + 3y \end{array}\right].\end{alignat*}$$

Solution: The matrix is

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}A = \left[\begin{array}{cc} -2& 1 \\ -5& 3 \end{array}\right],\end{alignat*}$$

which row reduces to the identity. Therefore, $\displaystyle T$ is one-to-one and onto, and a basis for the image is

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}\left\{ \left[\begin{array}{c} -2 \\ -5 \end{array}\right], \left[\begin{array}{c} 1 \\ 3 \end{array}\right] \right\}.\end{alignat*}$$

The inverse matrix is

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}A^{-1} = \left[\begin{array}{cc} -3& 1 \\ -5& 2 \end{array}\right],\end{alignat*}$$

so a formula for $T^{-1}$ is

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}T^{-1}\left( \left[\begin{array}{c} x \\ y \end{array}\right] \right) = \left[\begin{array}{c} -3x + y \\ -5x + 2y \end{array}\right].\end{alignat*}$$

2.

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}T\left( \left[\begin{array}{c} a \\ b \\ c \\ d \end{array}\right] \right) = \left[\begin{array}{c} a + 2b - c \\ 2b + c - d \end{array}\right].\end{alignat*}$$

Solution: The matrix is

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}A = \left[\begin{array}{cccc} 1& 2& -1& 0 \\ 0& 2& 1& -1 \end{array}\right],\end{alignat*}$$

which row reduces to

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}\left[\begin{array}{cccc} 1& 0& -2& 1 \\ 0& 1& \frac{1}{2}& -\frac{1}{2} \end{array}\right].\end{alignat*}$$

Therefore, $\displaystyle T$ is not one-to-one, but it is onto. A basis for the kernel is

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}\left\{ \left[\begin{array}{c} 2 \\ -\frac{1}{2} \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right], \left[\begin{array}{cccc} -1& \frac{1}{2}& 0& 1 \end{array}\right] \right\},.\end{alignat*}$$

and a basis for the image is

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}\left\{ \left[\begin{array}{c} 1 \\ 0 \end{array}\right], \left[\begin{array}{c} 2 \\ 2 \end{array}\right] \right\}.\end{alignat*}$$

The matrix isn’t invertible since it isn’t square, so $T$ isn’t invertible either.

3.

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}T\left( \left[\begin{array}{c} x \\ y \\ z \end{array}\right] \right) = \left[\begin{array}{c} 2x + y - z \\ -3x - y \\ y - 3z \end{array}\right].\end{alignat*}$$

Solution: The matrix is

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}A = \left[\begin{array}{ccc} 2& 1& -1 \\ -3& -1& 0 \\ 0& 1& -3 \end{array}\right],\end{alignat*}$$

which row reduces to

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}\left[\begin{array}{ccc} 1& 0& 1 \\ 0& 1& -3 \\ 0& 0& 0 \end{array}\right].\end{alignat*}$$

Therefore, $\displaystyle T$ is neither one-to-one nor onto. A basis for the kernel is

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}\left\{ \left[\begin{array}{c} -1 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{array}\right] \right\},\end{alignat*}$$

and a basis for the image is

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}\left\{ \left[\begin{array}{c} 2 \\ -3 \\ 0 \end{array}\right], \left[\begin{array}{c} 1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right] \right\}.\end{alignat*}$$

The matrix isn’t invertible, so neither is $T$.

4.

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}T\left( x \right) = \left[\begin{array}{c} x \\ 2x \\ 3x \\ 0 \end{array}\right].\end{alignat*}$$

Solution: The matrix is

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}A = \left[\begin{array}{c} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \\ 0 \end{array}\right],\end{alignat*}$$

which row reduces to

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}\left[\begin{array}{c} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{array}\right].\end{alignat*}$$

Therefore, $\displaystyle T$ is one-to-one, but not onto, and a basis for the image is

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}\left\{ \left[\begin{array}{c} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \\ 0 \end{array}\right] \right\}.\end{alignat*}$$

The matrix isn’t invertible since it isn’t square, so $T$ isn’t invertible either.

Problems 5–8 concern the following situation. Suppose when pizzas sell for $p$ dollars each, New Haven residents want to buy

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}q = 10 - p + d\end{alignat*}$$

thousand pizzas per week, and pizza restaurants want to sell

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}q = p - 2 + s\end{alignat*}$$

thousand pizzas per week. The market settles at the equilibrium price, i.e. the one where these two expressions for $q$ are equal. The variables $d$ and $s$ are called shocks. If $d > 0$, buyers demand $d$ thousand more pizzas than usual, regardless of price (for example, because a new restaurant opened). If $s > 0$, restaurants are willing to sell $s$ thousand more pizzas, regardless of price. Both $d$ and $s$ can be negative.

5. Find the equilibrium price $p(d, s)$ and quantity $q(d, s)$ of pizzas sold (in thousands), both in terms of $d$ and $s$.

Solution: Like the problem statement says, we set the two expressions for $q$ equal:

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}10 - p + d &= p - 2 + s\\[4px]2p &= 12 + d - s\\[4px]p &= 6 + \frac{d}{2} - \frac{s}{2},\end{alignat*}$$

and so

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}q &= p - 2 + s\\[4px]&= 4 + \frac{d}{2} + \frac{s}{2}.\end{alignat*}$$

6. Let $T : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ be given by

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}T\left( \left[\begin{array}{c} d \\ s \end{array}\right] \right) = \left[ \begin{array}{c} p(d, s) - p(0, 0) \\ q(d, s) - q(0, 0) \end{array} \right],\end{alignat*}$$

i.e. the change in the equilibrium price and quantity when there are shocks $d$ and $s$. Find the matrix $A$ for $T$, and then find $A^{-1}$.

Solution: The actual formula for $T$ is

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}T\left( \left[\begin{array}{c} d \\ s \end{array}\right] \right) &= \left[\begin{array}{c} 6 + \frac{d}{2} - \frac{s}{2} - 6 \\ 4 + \frac{d}{2} + \frac{s}{2} - 4 \end{array}\right]\\[4px]&= \frac{1}{2} \left[\begin{array}{c} d - s \\ d + s \end{array}\right],\end{alignat*}$$

so its matrix is

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}A = \frac{1}{2} \left[\begin{array}{cc} 1& -1 \\ 1& 1 \end{array}\right].\end{alignat*}$$

The inverse matrix is

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}A^{-1} = \left[\begin{array}{cc} 1& 1 \\ -1& 1 \end{array}\right].\end{alignat*}$$

7. Economists typically can’t observe shocks directly, only prices and quantities. If the price of pizza in New Haven rose by $\$1$ last week and the number of pizzas sold fell by 3 thousand, what shocks occurred?

Solution: This is solving the equation

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}A\left[\begin{array}{c} d \\ s \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array}{c} 1 \\ -3 \end{array}\right],\end{alignat*}$$

so

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}\left[\begin{array}{c} d \\ s \end{array}\right] &= A^{-1} \left[\begin{array}{c} 1 \\ -3 \end{array}\right]\\[4px]&= \left[\begin{array}{c} -2 \\ -4 \end{array}\right].\end{alignat*}$$

Restaurants were willing to sell 4000 fewer pizzas this week than usual, and buyers were willing to buy 2000 fewer.

8. Suppose you only have data on quantities, not prices. Let $Q : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ be defined by

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}Q\left( \left[\begin{array}{c} d \\ s \end{array}\right] \right) = q(d, s) - q(0, 0),\end{alignat*}$$

so its matrix is just the second row of the matrix for $T$. Find a basis for $\ker Q$. Explain in context what the kernel means, and specifically why it is a problem for determining shocks.

Solution: The matrix for $\displaystyle Q$ is

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}B = \left[\begin{array}{cc} \frac{1}{2}& \frac{1}{2} \end{array}\right],\end{alignat*}$$

which is already row-reduced (with only one row, there are no row operations to perform). Therefore, a basis for $\ker Q$ is

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}\left\{ \left[\begin{array}{c} -1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right] \right\}.\end{alignat*}$$

The kernel is the set of all shocks $(d, s)$ for which the equilibrium quantity sold doesn’t change. Having a nontrivial kernel is an issue for determining shocks, because it’s exactly the changes in shocks that are not detectable if we can only measure the equilibrium quantity: since

$$\begin{alignat*}{99}Q\left( \left[\begin{array}{c} d \\ s \end{array}\right] + \left[\begin{array}{c} -1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]t \right) &= Q\left( \left[\begin{array}{c} d \\ s \end{array}\right] \right) + Q\left(\left[\begin{array}{c} -1 \\ 1 \end{array}\right]t \right)\\[4px]&= Q\left( \left[\begin{array}{c} d \\ s \end{array}\right] \right)\end{alignat*}$$

for any $t$, an opposite supply and demand shock will not change $q$, and so we cannot completely determine the shocks from quantity alone.
